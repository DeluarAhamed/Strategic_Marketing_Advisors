The importance of checking your givens

The assumptions you never question are usually the ones costing you the most. Audit your givens before you audit anything else.

David A. Scarborough

April 13, 2026

In aviation, we were trained never to trust a "given." A reading that looks obvious, an assumption everyone shares — those are exactly the things that get people into trouble. The same discipline applies to business.

What is a given?

A given is any belief you've stopped questioning: "Our customers won't pay more." "That channel doesn't work for us." "This is just how our industry operates." Some givens are true. Many are simply old assumptions wearing the costume of fact.

1

List the load-bearing beliefs

Write down the assumptions your current strategy quietly depends on.

2

Date each one

Ask when each was last tested against real evidence — not memory or habit.

3

Test the most expensive

Pick the costliest assumption and design a small, cheap experiment to challenge it.

The most dangerous assumptions are the ones you no longer notice you're making.

A pilot's habit worth stealing

Before every flight: verify, don't assume. Run the same checklist on your business and you'll find money hiding in plain sight.

Checking your givens is uncomfortable — it means admitting you might be wrong. But it's also where the biggest, cheapest wins usually hide.

Run an assumption inventory

List the statements your plan treats as facts: who the best customer is, why people buy, which channel works, what price the market accepts, and what your team can deliver. Mark each as proven, partly proven, or inherited. Inherited beliefs deserve immediate scrutiny.

Design the smallest useful test

Do not debate an assumption for weeks when a small test can answer it. Interview five customers, change one landing page, call ten lost prospects, or price a pilot differently. Define in advance what evidence would change your mind.

Create a quarterly challenge session

Ask a person who was not involved in the original decision to challenge the givens behind your plan. Reward useful dissent. The purpose is not to undermine confidence; it is to prevent confidence from hardening into expensive blindness.

Common mistakes that reduce ROI

  • Treating past success as permanent proof
  • Asking questions that only confirm the preferred answer
  • Collecting data without defining a decision
  • Refusing to retire a tactic because of sunk cost

Frequently asked questions

Which assumptions are most dangerous?

Assumptions about customer motivation, unit economics, differentiation, and team capacity have the greatest ability to distort an entire plan.

How much evidence is enough?

Enough to make the next reversible decision. Use more evidence as the cost and irreversibility of the decision increase.

What if the team disagrees?

Write the competing assumptions down and test them against the same success measure rather than resolving the issue through seniority or volume.
