Perception: if we only knew what they were thinking

Your brand isn't what you say it is — it's what your customers believe. Here's how to understand their perception and shape it in your favor.

Mary Eule Scarborough

June 1, 2026

Every business owner believes they know what their customers think. Most are wrong — not because they aren't paying attention, but because perception is built from a thousand small signals the owner never sees.

Your brand is not the logo, the tagline, or the mission statement on your wall. It is the sum of every impression a customer collects: the speed of your reply, the tone of your invoice, the way your phone is answered, the photo on your profile. Perception is the reality you are actually competing in.

Start by listening, not broadcasting

Before you spend another dollar trying to change what people think, invest the time to learn what they already think. The answers are usually closer than you expect.

1

Mine your reviews

Read every review — yours and your competitors' — and note the words customers repeat. That language is your real positioning.

2

Interview both sides

Ask your best customers why they chose you, and your lost prospects why they didn't. The contrast is where the insight lives.

3

Watch the repeat questions

The questions that come up again and again reveal the exact gaps in your perceived value.

You can't manage a perception you haven't bothered to measure.

Close the gap deliberately

Once you understand the gap between how you want to be seen and how you are actually seen, you can close it on purpose. That might mean sharper messaging, faster responses, better photos, or simply telling a story you've been too modest to tell.

The SMA take

We start every engagement with a perception audit — what the market already believes about you — because you can't move a number you haven't measured.

Perception is malleable — but only for the businesses willing to look honestly at it first. Do the work, and you stop guessing what your customers are thinking. You start shaping it.

Build a practical perception scorecard

Turn vague impressions into evidence. Score the touchpoints a prospect experiences before, during, and after contacting you: search results, reviews, website clarity, response time, proposals, onboarding, delivery, and follow-up. Use a simple one-to-five scale for trust, relevance, ease, and differentiation. The lowest scores identify where perception is leaking revenue.

Test the message before scaling it

Before buying more traffic, run small message tests. Change one promise, proof point, or call to action at a time and compare qualified inquiries, booked conversations, and close rate. A message that earns more clicks but worse prospects is not an improvement. The goal is profitable alignment between what customers expect and what you deliver.

Measure the commercial effect

Track branded search volume, direct traffic, review sentiment, lead-to-meeting rate, proposal acceptance, and referrals. These indicators reveal whether the market understands and trusts your position. Review them monthly and pair the numbers with actual customer language so the dashboard never becomes detached from reality.

Common mistakes that reduce ROI

  • Trying to fix perception with a new logo alone
  • Copying a competitor's language instead of owning a specific strength
  • Ignoring operational signals such as slow replies or confusing proposals
  • Measuring awareness without measuring lead quality and revenue

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to change brand perception?

Visible improvements can appear within weeks when the problem is tactical, but meaningful market repositioning usually requires several months of consistent messaging and delivery.

Should a small business conduct formal research?

Yes, but it can be lightweight. Ten customer interviews, a review audit, lost-prospect feedback, and search-result analysis often reveal more than a large generic survey.

What should we fix first?

Start with the highest-friction touchpoint closest to revenue, usually response speed, offer clarity, proof, or the sales conversation.
